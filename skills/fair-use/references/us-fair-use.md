# US framework: evaluate the use, not a visual score

Reference review: 2026-09-07. US law only. Recheck current primary sources for a tailored assessment. The questions and examples below are assessment prompts, not bright-line legal rules.

## Rights and exceptions

[17 USC §§102, 106 and 107](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html) distinguish protected expression, exclusive rights and fair use. Ideas and methods are outside copyright protection, while reproducing or adapting protected expression can implicate an owner's rights. Being a creative adaptation does not itself resolve authorization. Fair use considers purpose/character, nature of the source, amount/substantiality, and market effect together.

Do not skip the earlier ownership/license/public-domain inquiry. Establish what material and act are at issue: e.g. copying a photo as an input, sharing the resulting illustration, or licensing it for an advertisement. These may present different questions.

## Four factors

| Factor | Questions to investigate | Common error to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose and character | What does the use actually do? Is it commentary on this work, analysis, teaching, a parody, illustration, decoration, or a competing product? What different purpose or character is supported? How does commercial use affect the analysis? | Equating a new medium, AI restyling, new meaning, a school context, or a nonprofit label with automatic fair use |
| Nature of the source | Is the material factual or highly expressive? Published or unpublished? What expressive choices matter here? | Treating a photograph as merely a fact, or unpublished status as an absolute bar |
| Amount and substantiality | How much protected material is taken, including its most significant elements? What is reasonably needed for this specific purpose? Can the purpose be achieved with less? | A fixed percentage, word count, pixel count or number-of-edits rule; assuming a whole image can never be justified |
| Market effect | Could this use substitute for the work or its relevant licensing uses? What evidence supports market harm, including if similar conduct became widespread? | Treating absence of current profit or an owner's unhappiness as the entire market analysis |

The [Copyright Office overview](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/more-info.html) describes case-specific balancing and recognizes that commercial/noncommercial character and the amount used are contextual. Its [FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-fairuse.html) rejects a fixed safe amount and cautions that changing another person's work does not by itself establish a right to claim it. Credit is not a substitute for a legal basis.

For each factor, connect actual evidence to arguments on both sides. Mark missing facts. Then synthesize the interaction; four checkbox answers are not an assessment. An artist's objection or hypothetical fee cannot alone make every critical use unlawful. Conversely, calling a commercial image “commentary” cannot replace examination of the actual work and use.

“Found online” does not by itself settle legal publication under §101. Investigate distribution facts when publication matters; otherwise mark publication status unknown rather than treating a public display as necessarily published.

## Warhol: why the use matters

In [Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith, 598 US 508 (2023)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-869_87ad.pdf), the Supreme Court addressed the first factor for a specific commercial licensing use of Orange Prince. It held that factor did not favor the Foundation. New expression or message did not settle the question where the challenged use shared the photograph's purpose of illustrating a magazine story about Prince. Do not characterize this narrow holding as deciding every use of the Prince Series, every remix, or every fair-use factor. See the majority opinion's introduction and discussion of the challenged use, rather than relying on a visual comparison alone.

## Illustrative branches, not verdicts

For parody and commerciality, consult [Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, 510 US 569 (1994)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep510/usrep510569/usrep510569.pdf). The Court reversed and remanded rather than treating commercial character as automatically barring fair use; inspect its reasoning about parody, borrowing and market effects. Do not call every humorous image a protected parody.

For significant excerpts and first-publication interests, consult [Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises, 471 US 539 (1985)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep471/usrep471539/usrep471539.pdf). Its facts concern unauthorized prepublication excerpts and a displaced licensing arrangement; it is not a universal prohibition on unpublished-source fair use. Read it alongside the current §107 text and subsequent relevant decisions.

These are issue-specific research starting points. Retrieve relevant circuit-specific authorities when jurisdiction or an actual dispute makes them material; this file is not a complete case-law survey.

- A critic reproduces a poster while analyzing its particular composition. The connection between the reproduction and the criticism may support a different-purpose argument. Examine amount, presentation, source and market as well.
- A seller restyles that poster into a watercolor and sells decorative prints. Different appearance alone gives little explanation of why borrowing the expression is justified for that use. Inspect what was retained and the relevant market.
- An artist creates an independent scene using a general technique associated with a movement. First identify whether specific protected expression was borrowed; do not force a fair-use analysis based only on shared technique.
- A student uses an entire image in a public portfolio. Investigate the actual presentation and promotional purpose; being a student does not settle the assessment.
- A purported parody borrows a source to comment on something else. Examine whether and why this particular borrowing is justified; the word “parody” alone is not evidence.

These are invented examples of questions to ask. They are not precedents and do not establish that the resulting work is lawful or infringing.
